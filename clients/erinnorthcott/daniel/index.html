The story

Sibling Love

What it is to be each other’s home.

Chapter One: Baby Brother

I was three years old the first time I met my brother. I’m told that I had been asking for a brother with such insistence that my parents, who hadn’t planned on having another child so soon, were eventually persuaded by me to try sooner. I was so certain of the coming of my brother that I told my parents, before they left for the hospital: “If it’s not a boy, then it’s the wrong baby. Don’t bring it home.”

Luckily for us all, they had a boy. And when he arrived, my whole world felt complete, and it stayed that way for a long time. I was barely more than a toddler myself, but I carried him everywhere, the way you carry something that already feels like yours to protect. We were close straight through childhood.

Chapter Two: Two Houses

Then our parents’ marriage came apart, and somewhere in that stress, my brother and I started taking it out on each other too. When the divorce settled, he went to live with our mom and I went to live with our dad: he was nine, I was twelve. For years after that we mostly saw each other on weekends, trading one house for another.

Our parents remarried, we gained step-siblings, and we lived by a schedule as to where was “home.” Our parents wanted child-free days to build their new relationships, which we understood and were happy for them, and which also left us without a place that was simply, unconditionally ours to call home on every day of the week.

Chapter Three: The Pact

I moved to Los Angeles at eighteen; Dan was fifteen. In my last year of college and Dan’s last year of high school, we started having these long-distance calls. These were the days when talking a couple of nights a week could cost over $250 a month, so after the first month we tried to limit it, but it was clear we craved the closeness we sensed only siblings can have.

We shared the lyrics to the songs we loved, lots of the first few albums of Ben Harper and Dave Matthews. Daniel sent me his spoken word pieces; I sent him some of mine that were so clearly not as awesome. We talked about the homeless feeling we’d both felt at times, and we made a pact: we would be each other’s home. Our homes would always be open to each other.

We started talking about seeing the world. We were both afraid to travel, and penniless to do so.

Chapter Four: The Living Room

A year later, he showed up in LA and turned my living room into his bedroom for about a year. The pact felt hard to keep at times, as Daniel was a mad scientist and not exactly neat, and my boyfriend at the time was not exactly supportive of our pact, no matter how I tried to explain it. But we were in our twenties, and somehow it just worked.

For the year he spent in my living room, Daniel worked as a driver to a budding actress, and I worked as a nanny to a family in Beverly Hills. It wasn’t easy for us to pay my rent and save, but eventually we had enough money to buy plane tickets to Taiwan. We’d need to find work there very soon after landing, as our spending money was hardly enough to get by for a week or two, but the world would open to us from there. We could feel it, and it was terrifying. This was before social media; we couldn’t see other young people like us traveling across to foreign lands.

Chapter Five: Taiwan

Later I did the same to him, showing up in Taiwan and staying with him for a month while I found a job and a place of my own. I’m sure that wasn’t easy for him either.

We were not easy on each other, exactly. We had an unconditional love and loyalty that flowed between us like a roaring river, and shared values, but we lived at completely different speeds. He was willing to take risks I never would have. He was passionate, sometimes explosive, and my instinct to protect him as the older sibling was never welcome. We fought.

After those two years, one in LA and one in Taipei, we had done it. We were very close and never wanted to live together again, in the most lovingly healthy way. But we were still each other’s homes, and our living rooms stayed open. We were the only two people alive who carried the same story, and there is a particular kind of freedom in knowing someone will always catch you. It let us both take chances, move to new places, try on new lives. There was always at least a couch to land on. I landed on his more than once, and he, the bigger risk taker, landed on mine many more times.

Chapter Six: To Build a Home

When Daniel got sick, my life simply stopped, the way it always had whenever he needed me. But this wasn’t as simple as an emergency room, a ride home from somewhere random, a breakup, or a big life change. This time it stopped for eleven months, and in parts of me for eternity. I was there for him. I told him to let me be his body in the outside world while he was stuck in the hospital undergoing treatment for acute myeloid leukemia. When nothing worked and it felt like the highest road was to let him go, we made it beautiful and held him all the way through for the ending he wanted: to die at home, and to be laid to rest in a forest.

In the last months of his life Daniel played Patrick Watson’s To Build a Home over and over again. His dying was inevitable, but we never talked about it like that. We left space for a miracle all the way until his last breath. When his body stopped, it was his life and the bond we built together that became the miracle. Our coming to terms with his death only happened through that song. That was Dan. That was us. Two people who chose, over and over, to be each other’s home.

Chapter Seven: Still Held

For years after his death I felt like I could fall so easily. I felt like everything was unstable and untrustable. Then I began to make his film, and though it took eleven years, I finally saw it released into the world. Though it will never be the same, I began to trust I might not fall all the way down, and with a healthy mix of caution and awe, I’ve allowed myself to feel held by the miracle and wild adventure of my life.
